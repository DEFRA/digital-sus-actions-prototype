7d. Choose providers with resilient infrastructure

Resilient public services are those designed to withstand and rapidly recover from the impacts of these events, as well as cyber attacks and others threats, ensuring that there are no significant breaks in service availability for citizens. However, the flipside to the question of resilient digital public services is the environmental impact of resources we put into backups and redundancy, and that is a trade-off we need to carefully strike. As Mark Butcher described it, appropriate resourcing for digital services is not something that the industry as whole has gotten right to this point: ‘We are wasting half of every pound we spend on IT’ at present, building services that are ‘over-sized and over-resourced.’ In order to make informed decisions about the appropriate levels of redundancy and resilience for public services, he argued ‘we need better data on risks, carbon and usage, so we can understand wastage.’

Resilience plans for data centres are strategies that are designed to keep facilities operating continuously (or recover them rapidly) when events like power failures, cyberattacks, natural disasters, or equipment breakdowns occur.

The supplier has or commits to implementing physical and non-physical solutions (‘adaptation solutions’) that substantially reduce the most important physical climate risks that are material to the service.

Choose providers with robust resilience plans that include climate events

(i) Choose providers incorporating resilience into site selection and design

Site selection and design has been done to factor climate resilience into planning. This could include:

Map risks of flooding, high temperatures, wildfires, and water scarcity at the site level

Site Selection and Design • Asset-Level Climate Data: Use hyper-local physical climate risk analysis—rather than country-level averages—to map flooding, high temperatures, wildfires, and water scarcity across short distances. • Scenario Testing: Model multiple time horizons and high-emissions climate trajectories during the pre-construction phase to identify critical points where equipment or cooling will fail. • Elevation and Physical Hardening: Elevate critical electrical and mechanical equipment above projected flood lines, and build structures to withstand high wind speeds, storms, and heavy precipitation. • Standards Compliance: Design facilities using frameworks like EN50600 Availability Classes (Classes 1–4) and ASHRAE environmental thermal envelopes

(ii) cooling and power infrastructure to handle shit

Uninterruptible power supply (UPS) systems are backup devices that provide emergency power if the main utility electricity fails. In data centres, these can ensure that critical servers and services do no go offline if there is a power failure.

Choose hosting providers with proportionate uninterruptible power supply systems to avoid excess energy waste while maintaining resilience.

Cooling and Thermal Management • Heatwave Adaptation: Upgrade cooling infrastructure—including liquid cooling, closed-loop systems, or higher-capacity chillers—to handle sustained, record-breaking summer temperatures without dropping efficiency. • Water-Scarcity Redundancy: Transition away from water-intensive evaporative cooling methods in drought-prone regions, or install on-site water recycling and storage buffers. • Real-Time Analytics: Deploy automated energy intelligence and sensor networks to dynamically adjust thermal workloads across GPUs and servers during sudden temperature spikes. Power and Grid Independence • On-Site Generation & Storage: Supplement unstable grid connections with dedicated backup generators, Uninterruptible Power Supplies (UPS), and co-located renewable energy or battery storage systems. • Geographic Redundancy: Distribute mission-critical services across geographically decoupled zones to prevent single-region failures during correlated climate events.
