8. Hardware

Hardware decisions can significantly affect sustainability across the whole lifecycle, from raw material extraction and manufacture to energy use, maintenance, reuse, repair and end-of-life recycling or disposal.

The UK Government's Critical Minerals Strategy sets out a 10-year vision for securing the materials needed to drive economic growth and the clean energy transition. This includes 20% sourced from recycling and the circular economy.

Circularity for hardware

Circularity means minimising our demand for primary resources and preventing unnecessary resource consumption and waste generation.

Circularity can be achieved through following the 4 principles of the Circular and Fair ICT Pact:

Buy Less
Ensure that we are only purchasing new hardware and software when it is clear that existing products cannot do the job
Buy Better
If new products are necessary, then ensure that we are procuring or developing energy-efficient, durable items that are designed to be repaired, upgraded and re-used
Use Better
Optimising the use of ICT products through their functional life, through proper maintenance, upgrades and sharing
Use Longer
Extending the lifespan of devices or software to avoid premature disposal, through repair and refurbishment, and sharing to keep in use

Hardware and sustainability

IT Hardware has very significant ‘embodied’ environmental and social impacts coming from the sourcing of raw materials, processing, manufacture, distribution and disposal of equipment at the end of its life. These go beyond carbon to include things like:

  • freshwater use
  • eutrophication
  • resource depletion
  • human and ecosystem toxicity
  • worker exploitation and modern slavery
  • land use change, including deforestation
  • biodiversity loss

Cloud services, AI and software all need hardware to run. Doing less of these things can indirectly reduce the need for more hardware.
