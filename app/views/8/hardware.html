We need to go beyond carbon where we can! LCAs may cover other environmental impacts?

Professor Deborah Andrews highlighted that for digital hardware, measuring carbon alone may ‘at best’ be ‘indicative’ of ‘the whole environmental impacts’ of that device, but at worst, it can be misrepresentative. She suggested that, as a rule of thumb, it’s fair to think that carbon (greenhouse gases) from the whole lifecycle represents about 50% of the environmental impact of a product, with the other 50% coming from the materials, through the pollution, resource consumption, land use change etc associated with the mining, processing and manufacturing processes. As Ryan Bark added, while we might not always be able to precisely quantify all of the environmental and social impacts of hardware, we can still ‘tell a story’ about the likely balance, and this is important as it helps raise awareness.

Envieon,ental benefits - energy efficient devices

• Carbon Reduction: Lowers carbon emissions by 44% compared to buying brand-new hardware. • Resource Preservation: Avoids the extraction of roughly 51,000 tonnes of raw materials and saves an estimated 12,000 megalitres of water. • Cost Savings: Delivers up to 40% financial savings, allowing funds to be reinvested into frontline services

Environmental impacts IT Hardware has very significant ‘embodied’ environmental impacts coming from the sourcing of raw materials, processing, manufacture, distribution and disposal of equipment at the end of its life. These go beyond carbon to include things like: • freshwater use • eutrophication • resource depletion • human and ecosystem toxicity • land use change (including deforestation) • biodiversity loss Cloud services, AI and software all need hardware to run. Doing less of these things can indirectly reduce the need for more hardware. Social impacts The supply chains for IT equipment may include exploitation or even modern slavery.

Circularity means minimising our demand for primary resources and preventing unnecessary resource consumption and waste generation. It can apply to hardware, software, architectural components and the design patterns we utilise for service journeys. Circularity can be achieved through following the 4 principles of the Circular and Fair ICT Pact: Buy Less: Ensure that we are only purchasing new hardware and software when it is clear that existing products cannot do the job Buy Better: If new products are necessary, then ensure that we are procuring or developing energy-efficient, durable items that are designed to be repaired, upgraded and re-used Use Better: Optimising the use of ICT products through their functional life, through proper maintenance, upgrades and sharing Use Longer: Extending the lifespan of devices or software to avoid premature disposal, through repair and refurbishment, and sharing to keep in use When products reach their end of life, then we should ensure that they are appropriately recycled.
