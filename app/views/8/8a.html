8a. Minimise the lifecycle impacts of hardware

The points in this section broadly follow the principles in this document: https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6ad7573b8ef5e8a9fc8ed5f5/Circular_Economy_Working_Group_Hardware_Lifecycle_Management_Toolkit.pdf/

(i) Adopt approaches to extend hardware lifecycles where possible

Extending the life of existing devices reduces the need for new purchase and the associated environmental impacts.

This can mean:

  • Maintain functionality; swap faulty; repair reserve stock
  • Appropriate security measures can have a direct positive impact on the operational lifecycle of IT hardware, protecting devices from malware, overheating, and premature component failure. They can also help to avoid carbon- and resource-intensive investigations and remedial actions.
  • Internal redeployment: If hardware is no longer needed by one team or project, then enable internal reuse of hardware through redeploying used equipment within department or across government. this could involve moving enterprise devices from demanding roles (e.g., engineering workstations) to users or tasks with lighter computational requirements

Instead of being discarded or sent to a landfill, this hardware is given an extended operational lifespan.

Reuse means using a product again for the same or a new purpose in its original form, without any major processing, repair, or disassembly

(i) Support backward and forward compatibility for service user devices

Backward compatibility means newer technology being designed to work with older data and systems. Forward compatibility refers to newer systems and processes that can integrate with older devices, processes and data formats.

Together, backward and forward compatability help ensure that older user devices can continue to be used and their replacement is not 'induced' by the development of new services and systems.

Support older user devices and operating systems by designing for low power and bandwidth and compatibility with existing data formats and software.

>

(ii) Secure replacement hardware only when there is a clear need

Rather than setting a fixed timeframe for device refresh (e.g. 3 years), only replace and update end-user devices when there is an evidenced need from users for this.

Avoiding unneccessary device refreshes means that the environemntal impacts associated with manufacturing new equipment are avoided.

Consider 'Device as a Service' (DaaS). This is a subscription model where organisations lease IT hardware, and this includes management of the end of life and disposal. This approach generally boosts sustainability through performance-based device refreshes, prioritising repair and refurbishment.

(ii) Rent or purchase refurbished or remanufactured equipment when required

'Second life' IT equipment refers to those that are reused and redeployed after finishing their initial primary lifecycle.

This involves some degree of restoration:

Second life devices can be refurbished or remanufactured:

Approach Description
Refurbishment Repairing and cleaning a used product to restore basic function and fix broken parts
Remanufacture A comprehensive process that strips down a device, replaces all worn or out-of-spec components, and rebuilds it to match or even exceed original factory specifications

If your service hires or purchases equipment, select remanufactured or refurbished devices where possible.

CO BENEFITS reduction in procurement costs - Cost Reduction: Businesses and consumers acquire business-grade equipment (like Dell, HP, or Lenovo hardware) at a fraction of the cost of buying brand-new.

(iii) If new equipment is required, ensure it meets relevant eco-label requirements

Efficient and sustainable hardware means that that is:

  • efficiently manufactured with recycled raw materials and social risks in supply chains reduced as far as possible
  • operates efficiently, minimising carbon and other emissions with electricity consumption

There are a number of global 'ecolabel' certifications for sustainable electronic devices, verifying performance across a range of factors including energy usage, whole lifecycle impacts, and social issues, from raw material extraction to manufacture and distribution.

In order of rgiour, 3 of the major certifications include:

Certification Description
TCO Covers categories of climate impacts, chemical usage, circularity and social and environmental risks in supply chains
EPEAT EPEAT (Electronic Product Environmental Assessment Tool): Evaluates the environmental impacts of devices across the full lifecycle, from material extraction to end-of-life, awarding gold, silver and bronze certifications
Energy Star Focusing exclusively on energy efficiency during active use and standby modes

When renting or purchasing new hardware, choose that that meets the highest ecolabel standards where possible.

In addition, Product Carbon Footprints (PCFs) or equivalents should be provided by the manufacturer. This quantifies the total greenhouse gas emissions generated across a product's lifecycle.

(iv) Apply the concept of e-waste compensation to hardware procurement

E-waste compensation is where the purchase or lease of a new electronic device is balanced by the collection and responsible recycling of an equivalent volume of old or broken hardware.

Choose suppliers offering e-waste compensation where possible.

(iv) Decomission devices sustainabily at end of life

If devices cannot be reused internally, but are still functioning, then prioritise reuse externally:

  • Re-market - sell functioning devices back to suppliers or remarketers
  • Certified Refurbishment and Resale: Sending used assets to specialized partners who wipe data, test components, repair faults, and resell them into the secondary market (often with a warranty).
  • Donate devices, where appropriate to charities, schools or public services or sell devices and donate funds. Check if closed loops are in place where the donation of hardware does not lead to additional e-waste elsewhere.

Prioritise more circular options at the end of hardware’s usable life

For hardware devices that have reached the end of their usable life, and that cannot be sold or reused, follow, from most to least desirable, the waste hierarchy applied to ICT equipment:

Approach Description
Reclaiming and upcycling components Processing devices that are beyond economic repair to harvest valubale components such as circuit boards, hard drives or displays for reuse, refurbishing other devices or sale
Recycling materials Processing devices that are beyond economic repair to recover materials such as gold, platinum, lithium, aluminium and copper that can then be used to manufacture new electronics, industrial goods, and infrastructure
Incineration with energy recovery Non-recyclable materials are burned at high temperatures to create steam, which spins turbines to generate electricity or supply district heating systems
Incineration without energy recovery Non-recyclable materials are burned but the heat generated is dissipated into the environment rather than captured for electricity or heating
Landfill Residual waste is placed into engineered or open-air pits where it slowly compacts over decades

Decommission legacy systems as sustainably as possible

If an existing service or process is being replaced, a project may involve the retirement of hardware such as servers or other devices.

Fully consider the end-of-life options for any hardware you will decommission in accordance with the waste hierarchy. Re-using and repairing over recycling.
