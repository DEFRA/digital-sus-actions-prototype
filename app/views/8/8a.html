8a. Extend the working life of hardware

The points in this section broadly follow the principles in this document:

Hardware Lifecycle Management Toolkit GDSA

Actions we can take to keep existing hardware in use,

Cuts manufacturing demand; up to 80% of a laptop’s lifetime carbon footprint occurs during manufacturing, not usage.

Reuse means using a product again for the same or a new purpose in its original form, without any major processing, repair, or disassembly

(i) Adopt approaches to extend hardware lifecycles

Extending the life of existing devices reduces the need for replacement hardware and its associated environmental impact coming from manufacture and disposal.

The following approaches can all have a positive impact on hardware lifecycles:

Aspect Description
Proactive device maintenance Ongoing maintenance through physical care and power protection, optimising battery management and encouraging staff to follow best practice in caring for end-user devices all contribute to maintaining the functionality of equipment
Security measures Appropriate security measures can protect devices from malware, overheating, and premature component failure. They can also help to avoid carbon- and resource-intensive investigations and remedial actions.
Redeploying hardware internally Having a 'hardware return loop' in place, where defective or unneeded equipment is collected, repaired or refurbished where appropriate, and then forms a 'reserve stock'. Devices can be redeployed within the department or across government. Older hardware may be reassigned to less critical, lower-demand tasks.

(ii) Support backward compatibility of new systems with older end-user devices

Ensuring backward compatibility for hardware means designing newer systems so legacy equipment can still work with it.

Backward compatability reduces hardware disposal by allowing users to keep using legacy devices even as as software is upgraded and new systems introduced. This helps ensure that the replacement of devices is not 'induced' unnecessarily, so reducing manufacturing demand.

When designing and developing new digital services or other projects, and whether users are external or internal, maximise backward compatibility by designing for low power and bandwidth and compatibility with existing data formats and software.

Co-benefits and trade-offs

Co-benefits

Alongside sustainability impact, right-sizing cloud resources brings co-benefits in terms of:

  • Cost: Keeping older devices in use longer avoids the 'switching cost' of securing a new piece of hardware

Trade-offs

Potential trade-offs may include:

  • The "Legacy Code" Tax: Maintaining backward compatibility requires systems to run bloated, inefficient code to support old formats. This can increase processing power and data center energy consumption. Energy Efficiency Gains vs. E-waste: Modern hardware is vastly more energy-efficient per watt than hardware from a decade ago. Sometimes, keeping an ancient, power-hungry server alive (high operational carbon) harms the planet more than replacing it with an ultra-efficient new one (high embodied carbon).
