2c. Reduce and simplify offline channels while maintaining inclusivity

Digitalisation can bring sustainability benefit as user interactions through digital channels are usually less resource intensive than those occurring offline through paper forms, or in-person and telephone interactions.

However, offline channels must be available as accessible alternatives for people who cannot use digital services.

Lead role families: Design Product & Delivery

Likely co-benefits: Accessibility, inclusivity

Read more about digital take-up and offline channels

Digital take-up

Digital take-up is a measure of the the percentage of people using services online in relation to other channels.

The GOV.UK Service Manual states that teams must plan to continually increase digital take-up, whilst still ensuring that offline channels are provided for those who lack access to digital technology or the skills to use it.

  • Measuring digital take-up, GOV.UK Service Manual
  • Provide a joined up experience across all channels, Point 3, GOV.UK Service Standard
  • The importance of creating inclusive government services, Ben Carpenter, Government Digital Service Blog

(i) Design to reduce consumption of paper, printing and postage

Paper is often used for physical documents that users fill out and submit to access services, as well as by staff supporting them.

Where appropriate, design services and processes to reduce consumption of paper and printing and postage through digitisation.

Consider that sometimes paper forms will be needed to support digitally excluded users. In some circumstances, staff supporting delivering of the service may also need paper. For example, vets conducting visits to farms as part of Defra services need to use paper rather than digital devices.

Co-benefits and trade-offs

Co-benefits

Alongside sustainability benefits, setting a page weight budget brings co-benefits in terms of:

  • Inclusivity: Budgets encourage close consideration of what is essential for users with small screen sizes or low bandwidth, helping ensure that pages work for users with slow connections

(ii) Design to reduce consumption of paper, printing and postage

(iii) Design to reduce travel, where appropriate

Services can require users or staff to travel.

Consider opportunities to reduce the need for user travel and in-person interactions, whilst understanding when/if face-to-face human interaction maybe be more effective, or needed alongside digital touchpoints to deliver desired policy outcomes.

These may be particularly important for services intended to shift behaviours (rather than those that are purely transactional), for example farmers signing up for new farming subsidies. For these types of services, human interactions can be an important way for keeping people engaged in the service journey, particularly where earning trust is critical to service delivery.

Robust social and user research (including ethnography) can help you better understand when digital and human touchpoints will be most effective in service delivery.

Where appropriate, allow people to make an informed decision about when to travel.
