There are a number of approaches we can take to help ensure that users’ complete their journeys at the first attempt, avoiding the resources required to support in order to complete their goals with a service.

The pace of journey also ensures that only appropriate resources are consumed.

(i) Design with established patterns

In design, a pattern is a reusable template or blueprint that solves a commonly recurring problem.

Patterns are commonly described at two different levels for services:

  • A design pattern is a general solution to a common problem in digital interaction design, such as the layout of particular screen or a sequence of screens
  • A service pattern provides a solution to a common problem in service design, such as a user task or stage of a service

Use of established and proven patterns, where appropriate, avoids unnecessary rework and saves on the resources required to develop and test a solution from scratch.

See also: 2b. Develop with modular components

Read more about implementing this point

GOV.UK Service Standard, Point 13

Point 13 of the GOV.UK Service Standard sets out the requirement for government services to 'Use and contribute to open standards, common components and patterns'

The GOV.UK Design System

The GOV.UK Design System ensures the consistency of digital pages of services hosted on GOV.UK. The system features a library of ‘components’ (individual elements of a user interface) and ‘patterns’ (best practice design solutions for specific user-focused tasks and page types) that can be reused by services.

The GOV Reuse Library

The GOV Reuse Library is a directory of departmental and public sector design systems, many of which build on the components and patterns in the GOV.UK Design System. The library also includes service patterns, design standards and content style guides.

Co-benefits and trade-offs

Co-benefits

Alongside sustainability benefits, designing with established patterns brings co-benefits in terms of:

  • Delivery time: Use of proven patterns reduces unnecessary rework and saves on delivery time
  • Usability and performance: Common design and service patterns are familiar for users, helping them quickly navigate and complete tasks
  • Accessibility: GOV.UK design patterns have already been tested for accessibility

(ii) Design an appropriately paced service

The pace of a service is the speed or rate it is delivered at. This covers response time, processing time and task completion time. Faster services may require more resources to deliver.

Consider how soon users need a response or outcome and if the pace of the service could be reduced. For example, if users need a response the same day or this could be delivered the next day.

Services with a slower pace often require fewer resources to deliver.
