1c. Design for digitilisation while balancing need for offline channels

User interactions through digital channels are usually less resource and carbon intensive than those occurring offline through paper forms, or in-person and telephone interactions.

Digitising service touchpoints may (often) save energy, resources and associated environmental impacts.

(i) Balance increasing digital take-up of services with the need for offline channels

Digital take-up is a measure of the the percentage of people using services online in relation to other channels.

The GOV.UK Service Manual states that teams must plan to continually increase digital take-up

However, we still need to ensure that offline channels are provided to allow users to complete their goals. 'Users should not be excluded or have an inferior experience because they lack access to technology or the skills to use it.'

Ensuring that different channels are joined up helps users to complete their goals with a service at the first attempt.

(ii) Design to reduce consumption of paper, printing and postage

Paper might be used for physical documents that users fill out and submit to access services, as well as by staff supporting the delivery of a service.

Reduce consumption of paper, printing and postage through digitisation where appropriate, whilst still providing offline channels for digitally excluded users.

(iii) Design to reduce consumption of paper, printing and postage

Paper might be used for physical documents that users fill out and submit to access services, as well as by staff supporting the delivery of a service.

Reduce consumption of paper, printing and postage through digitisation where appropriate, whilst still providing offline channels for digitally excluded users.

NB: Remember that sometimes paper will be needed to support digitally excluded users. In some circumstances, staff supporting delivering of the service may also need paper. For example, vets conducting visits to farms as part of Defra services need to use paper rather than tablets.

(iv) Design to reduce travel, where appropriate

Services can require users or staff to travel.

Consider opportunities to reduce the need for user travel and in-person interactions, whilst understanding when/if face-to-face human interaction maybe be more effective, or needed alongside digital touchpoints to deliver desired policy outcomes.

These may be particularly important for services intended to shift behaviours (rather than those that are purely transactional), for example farmers signing up for new farming subsidies. For these types of services, human interactions can be an important way for keeping people engaged in the service journey, particularly where earning trust is critical to service delivery.

Robust social and user research (including ethnography) can help you better understand when digital and human touchpoints will be most effective in service delivery.

Where appropriate, allow people to make an informed decision about when to travel.
