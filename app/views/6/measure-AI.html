Measuring progress

  • Measure the impacts of AI

    • Measuring the impact of AI

    Measuring the environmental impacts of Artificial Intelligence can:

    • Provide insights to practitioners working with AI, such as training models or integrating it into workflows, on the impact hotspots
    • Be integrated into broader whole-service measurement, alongside other aspects of digital services or projects

    Calculations for the energy use of AI can then be used to estimate carbon and water usage.

    Covering the whole lifecycle of AI technologies would mean covering the following three stages

    • Data preparation
    • Training
    • Inference
    However, most measurements of energy use focus either just on inference, or include training alongside inference.

    Measuring AI model energy use

    Different methodologies are appropriate for the measurement of the energy use of AI technologies, depending on where models are running. This can be

    • AI models accessed through public cloud
    • Local, self-hosted AI models
    • AI that is integrated into other software

    AI models accessed through public cloud

    AI that is consumed through APIs in the public cloud is generally paid for through input and output tokens.

    Approaches can include:

    Approach Description
    Direct Model APIs Call a provider’s proprietary API model (e.g., OpenAI, Anthropic, Google AI Studio) and pay for the input and output tokens consumed
    AI Model as a Service (AIaaS / MaaS) The delivery of raw or pretrained AI/ML models via cloud infrastructure and standardized API endpoints, which can be paid for by subscription or tokens
    Hyperscaler Managed AI Platforms Comprehensive enterprise suites integrated into major cloud ecosystems (e.g., Microsoft Azure AI Services, Amazon Bedrock, Google Cloud Vertex AI, Oracle Cloud OCI Generative AI)

    Estimating the energy use of AI models through the public cloud can be calculated through

    • Using tokens to estimate energy consumption for the inference phase
    • Top-down methods utilising cloud provider billing for training and inference

    Using tokens to estimate energy use

    Energy usage for generative AI models in the inference phase is sometimes reported ‘per query’. However, queries can range greatly in length and complexity, while tokens usages serves to count how much work an AI model is actually doing.

    Tokens are divided into:

    Input Tokens
    Everything you feed into the model before it generates an answer. This can include your prompt, system instructions, chat history, attached documents, code snippets, and background data.
    Output tokens
    The response or content the model generates for you. This includes the final answer, summary, table, code output, as well as hidden "reasoning" (thinking) tokens.

    Energy consumption scales directly with the number of tokens processed, and generating an output token takes significantly more energy than processing an input token.

    Token usage data should be available through provider dashboards and billing reports, and there are a number of tools we can use to estimate the energy use of input and output tokens.

    Tool Description
    CLEER Dashboard A public tool created by the Sustainable AI Group that models and estimates the per-token energy consumption for both proprietary and open source models
    ML.Energy Dashboard A benchmarking and tracking tool that measures, analyses, and exposes the energy and resource consumption of modern machine learning and generative AI models, including electricity consumption per generated token or chain-of-thought step

    Published estimates of per-token energy use

    Published research from the IEA and Epoch AI, combined with GPU inference cost analysis, puts a large frontier model somewhere around 3 to 4 watt-hours per thousand tokens.

    Self-hosted AI models

    Self-hosting an AI model means running it on hardware that the organisation owns or rents, or as dedicated instances on exclusive single-tenant infrastructure. This could be:

    • local machines such as personal computers, high-end laptops with GPUs, or other edge devices
    • a server rack in on- and off-premises data centres
    • virtual machines on private cloud instances

    The AI models that are self-hosted will generally be open-weight.

    Measurement of the energy use of self-hosted models generally requires a bottom-up approach. The two main approaches to bottom-up model-level estimation are:

    • Direct measurement of energy use via telemetry
    • Hardware profiling
    • Token usage to estimate energy consumption

    Direct measurement of the energy use of AI models can occur through tracking real-time power draw on hardware and use of software frameworks to track and calculate energy consumed during tasks like model training or local inference.

    Direct measurement of energy use via telemetry (for training and inference phases)

    Modern processors, GPUs, and AI accelerators feature hardware performance counters that track operational events, which can be mapped to energy consumption. At a higher scale, physical hardware probes or Power Distribution Unit (PDU) meters measure power at the rack or server level.

    See page for on- and -off premises measurement for more detail on these approaches

    Use of software instrumentation

    There are a number of software frameworks can be used to track hardware usage logs during training or inference runs to log energy consumption. Some examples of software tools that can be used for AI are:

    Software tool Description
    EcolagM Estimate and track the environmental footprint of Generative Al models at inference
    EcoLogits An open-source Python library that tracks and calculates the environmental and energy impacts of generative AI models in real time when accessed through APIs
    CodeCarbon Can monitor power consumption across the CPU, GPU, and RAM.
    Carbontracker Can be used to track power usage across specific hardware components such as Graphical Processor Units (GPUs), Central Processing Units (CPUs) and RAM.
    MIT's EnergAIzer EnergAIzer uses predictive modeling to forecast how much electricity an AI workload will draw. It analyzes the specific parameters of an AI task (such as training or inference jobs running on GPUs) and estimates power demands beforehand.
    Experiment Impact Tracker The Experiment Impact Tracker is a Python library designed to measure real-time energy use, hardware resource usage, and carbon emissions during machine learning training
    ! Warning NOTE: Tools may use different system boundaries, assumptions and carbon intensity data. Record the tool, version, configuration and date used.

    AI that is integrated into other software

    AI capabilities can be built directly into user-facing software applications like CRM, customer support, or productivity suites.

    For AI that is integrated into broader software solutions and hosted in cloud services or software-as-a-service (SaaS), estimating the energy use of AI models requires that reporting be made available by suppliers. This could include:

    AI Energy Score (or other benchmark)

    The supplier provides the AI Energy Score (or an equivalent industry-standard energy efficiency benchmark) for the proposed AI model(s).

    Energy use

    The supplier reports on the energy use of AI models.

    Lifecycle analysis (LCA)

    The supplier provides an attributional LCA for the end-to-end lifecycle of the proposed AI model(s) verified in accordance with ISO 14040 and 14044.

    The functional unit for this LCA should include the proposed AI model(s) and the associated data, hardware, networking and infrastructure assets over any development, training and deployment phases.

    The LCA should include in its scope:

    A - Raw material acquisition

    B - Production Devices

    • production and assembly
    • manufacturer support activities
    • production of support equipment
    • ICT-specific site construction

    C - Use

    • training data acquisition, processing and storage
    • model training, including R&D compute, retraining and finetuning
    • model inference service, including active compute, networking, idle tasks, storage and cooling
    • network transfer of requests to inference and the end user
    • use of support equipment
    • operator support activities
    • service provider support activities

    D - End of life

    • preparation of ICT goods for reuse
    • storage / disassembly / dismantling / crushing

    The LCA should, at minimum, provide metrics on:

    • energy consumption (Wh)
    • greenhouse gas emissions (gCO2-e)
    • water consumption (L or m3)
    • abiotic resource depletion (kgSb-e)

    In the absence of lifecycle analysis

    In the absence of a lifecycle analysis, the supplier provides real metrics, or verified estimates, for the sustainability of the following phases of the AI lifecycle for the proposed AI model(s):

    • the acquisition, processing and storage of training data
    • the training phase, including R&D compute, retraining and finetuning required for the current model version
    • the inference service on a defined set of hardware, measured as a median per token (or per inference if the model is not tokenised)
    • the embodied impacts from sourcing, manufacturing, transportation and end-of-life of hardware used for the above

    Metrics offered include, but are not limited to:

    • energy consumed (Wh)
    • greenhouse gas emissions (gCO2-e)
    • water consumed (L or m3)
    • abiotic resource depletion for minerals and metals (kgSb-e)
