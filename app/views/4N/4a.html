4a. Design for sustainable architectural patterns (mention circularity?) (design for modularity, portability and flexibility)

Where appropriate, designing for more modular architecture, and workloads that can be decoupled from underlying hardware and infrastructure, bringing benefits in terms of avoiding over-provisioning infrastructure flexibile and portable means that we can maximise efficiencies for sustainability and cost benefit. and to be flexible workloads enable the sustainable operation of a digital service, including porting workloads between cloud locations, and enabling carbon-aware computing, and running workloads when there is unused capacity or when the grid is greener

NB There may be constraints on how flexible workloads can be, and not appropraite for many types of workload.

Defining modular design for architcture: a design approach that divides a system into independent, self-contained, and reusable units (modules) that interact through well-defined interfaces and protocols.

(i) Design for 'modular' architectural patterns over traditional monolithic systems

Traditional monolithic architecture is where an application is built, compiled, and deployed as a single, self-contained unit.

More modular patterns, such as modular monoliths and microservice architectures, break monolithic systems down into separate components.

These approaches enable sustainability benefits through:

  • independent scaling of services, more efficient allocation of compute resources and reduced energy consumption
  • modularity aids the reuse of components, Using existing, proven, components saves on the resources associated with developing and testing these from scratch.
Approach Description
Modular monoliths A design pattern that structures a single, unified application into independent, loosely coupled logical modules
Microservice architecture A pattern that breaks applications into smaller, independent services. This helps with the build of composable and collaborative services, driven by events, commands and queries.

See also 'Serverless' and 'Resuse existing components'

Co-benefits and trade-offs

Co-benefits

Alongside sustainability impact, modular approaches bring co-benefits in terms of:

  • Development Speed: Modular development supports faster delivery and adaptability to change
  • Resilience: Resilience is improved as a fault in one module or service does not impact the whole system
  • Cost: Microservice architectures support independent scaling of services and efficient cost allocation

Trade-offs

Potential trade-offs may include:

  • Resource usage: Communication between microservices can lead to increased energy consumption due to communication between services
  • Complexity: Microservice approaches can increase architectural and operational complexity through deployment and monitoring

(ii) Design for workload portability

Portable workloads are those where applications, data, and services can be moved between computing environments with minimal code refactoring or configuration changes.

This requires that workloads not be reliant on any single environment or underlying infrastructure, and then 'porting' can occur between public and private cloud, on-premises or even between different providers.

This approach can bring sustainability benefit by:

  • enabling general efficiencies and reduced energy consumption
  • running workloads in locations where more low carbon electricity is available on the grid.

Approaches that can help enable portability include:

Approach Description
Use of containers Containarised architecture means packaging software into self-contained units that can then run in any environment
Automated container workflows Platforms such as Kubernetes can be used to automate the deployment, scaling, and management of containerized workloads
Following open standards Applications that are designed to follow open industry standards, rather than provider-specific standards, can more easily be ported between environments

Co-benefits and trade-offs

Co-benefits

Alongside sustainability impact, portable workloads bring co-benefits in terms of:

  • Cost: Increased flexibility in terms of workload placement allows optimising for cost, avoiding vendor lock-in
  • Resilience: Improved resilience by allowing workloads to be relocated or scaled across environments
  • Performance: Supports workload placement optimisation (e.g. region, provider, infrastructure type)

Trade-offs

Potential trade-offs may include:

  • Complexity: Portable workloads introduce additional architectural and operational complexity

(iii) Design for workload flexibility

Designing workloads for flexibility means moving away from 'real-time' models that aim to complete tasks as soon as possible, to those where computational demand can be dynamically shifted in time.

To be 'time-shiftable', workloads must not require that an immediate response be provided to a user.

These patterns can help maximise efficiency and avoid reliance on peak-capacity infrastructure, allowing workloads to be scheduled when there is unused capacity or the electricity grid is at a lower carbon intensity.

The approaches that can be taken to enable time-shifting include:

Approaches Description
Batch processing Grouping large volumes of data to be processed all at once, rather than one by one
Design interruptible workloads Designing workloads that can be paused, stopped, and resumed at any time without data loss

See points on temporal shifting, 4c

Co-benefits

Alongside sustainability impact, flexible workloads can bring co-benefits in terms of:

  • Cost: Aligning resources with actual usage optimises infrastructure costs
  • Scaling: Enables more efficient scaling strategies to be put in place

Trade-offs

Potential trade-offs may include:

  • Complexity: Flexible workloads introduce architectural complexity (e.g. queues, event handling, retry logic)
