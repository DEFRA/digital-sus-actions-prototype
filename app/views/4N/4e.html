4e. Maximise efficiency in on-premises data centres

On premises systems give control but...On-premise gives control, with potential cooling drawbacks

Take action to ensure that on-premises servers are running as efficiently as possible, reducing associated energy consumption.

An on-premises data centre is a physical computing facility owned, built, and managed directly by an organization within its own building or property

Lead role families: Architecture
Likely co-benefits Cost: Lower costs coming the storage of data

(i) Consolidate workloads

Consolidating workloads means combining multiple applications, databases, or virtual machines that previously ran on separate physical servers onto a smaller number of higher capacity servers.

This approach can increase hardware utilisation rates and reduce overall energy consumption.

(ii) Implement dynamic balancing

Dynamic balancing involves distributing network traffic in real-time across local servers based on current demand.

This approach can optimise power distribution and reduce overall energy consumption.

To maximise efficiency in an on-premises data centre, focus on optimizing airflow, consolidating workloads, upgrading physical infrastructure, and tracking resource metrics. [1, 2] Airflow and Cooling Optimisation • Hot and Cold Aisle Containment: Arrange server racks in alternating rows (hot-to-hot and cold-to-cold) and install physical barriers, curtains, or doors to prevent hot exhaust air from mixing with cool intake air. This can improve cooling efficiency by 20% to 50%. [1, 2] • Blanking Panels and Sealing: Install blanking panels in unused rack spaces and seal floor or ceiling gaps to stop chilled air from bypassing critical equipment. [1, 2] • Raise Operating Temperatures: Gradually increase room or server inlet temperatures within safe ASHRAE guidelines (saving roughly 4% to 5% in energy costs for every 1°F increase). [1, 2] • Advanced Cooling: Explore variable-speed fans, free-air cooling, or targeted liquid cooling for high-density hardware. [1, 2] Server Consolidation and Virtualisation • Virtualisation: Deploy hypervisors and virtual machines (VMs) to raise typical server utilisation rates from low legacy baselines (10%–15%) up to 50% or higher, reducing the total number of physical machines needed. [1, 2, 3] • Retire Zombie Servers: Audit and shut down underutilised, abandoned, or single-purpose legacy servers that consume power without delivering active workloads. [1, 2] • Hardware Refresh Cycles: Replace older servers, storage arrays, and network switches with modern, energy-proportioned units that deliver higher compute-per-watt ratios. [1, 2, 3] Power and Electrical Infrastructure • High-Efficiency UPS and PDUs: Upgrade Uninterruptible Power Supply (UPS) systems and Power Distribution Units (PDUs) to high-efficiency models (aiming for online UPS efficiencies of 95%–98%). [1, 2] • Dynamic Power States: Enable built-in processor power management features (such as C-states and P-states) so hardware scales power draw down during low-demand periods. [1, 2] • Facility Lighting: Replace legacy fluorescent bulbs with low-energy LEDs tied to motion sensors. [1, 2, 3] Monitoring and Management • Data Centre Infrastructure Management (DCIM): Implement DCIM tools to track real-time power, temperature, humidity, and rack-level usage. • Track Key Metrics: Regularly calculate and monitor Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) to measure how much total facility energy reaches the IT equipment versus overhead systems like HVAC. Guidance and best-practice frameworks are available via the US Department of Energy Best Practices Guide. [1, 2, 3, 4]
