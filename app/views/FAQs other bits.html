Not just efficiencies, but the principles of the circular economy can be appliued to digitals ervidesa and other projects too.

Circularity means minimising our demand for primary resources and preventing unnecessary resource consumption and waste generation. It can apply to hardware, software, architectural components and the design patterns we utilise for service journeys. Circularity can be achieved through following the 4 principles of the Circular and Fair ICT Pact: Buy Less: Ensure that we are only purchasing new hardware and software when it is clear that existing products cannot do the job Buy Better: If new products are necessary, then ensure that we are procuring or developing energy-efficient, durable items that are designed to be repaired, upgraded and re-used Use Better: Optimising the use of ICT products through their functional life, through proper maintenance, upgrades and sharing Use Longer: Extending the lifespan of devices or software to avoid premature disposal, through repair and refurbishment, and sharing to keep in use When products reach their end of life, then we should ensure that they are appropriately recycled.

NOES on measurement

We need to measure the environmental impacts of our services and the teams delivering them in order to be confident our actions are serving to reduce these. Publishing our approaches to measurement and our results is also a key step in developing a culture of transparency and learning. If you are not yet able to accurately measure you service's emissions, make a start with what you can do and the data you are able to access. This could include tools to help with estimating impacts, using proxy measures, or following the approach of best practice examples and case studies.

Measurement and the challenges around it

The availability of the right data for sustainability assessment is a challenge There were some stark words on the position the tech sector finds itself in on meaningful sustainability data. As Mark Butcher put it: ‘we are still in the world of measuring things with made up numbers…to go in reports that sit on shelves and no one does anything with.’ For Professor Andrews, a large part of the problem is that ‘many parties don’t want high quality data published on them’. Ewen Anderson described that the metrics that are readily available are often those that tech companies are keen to showcase ‘for marketing purposes’, what Ray Knight called the ‘feel good fluff’, rather than those we need to meaningfully assess sustainability impacts. Chris Adams suggested that government can also lead the way on improving this situation, setting standards on transparency, embedding demands on the right data into contracts and publishing case studies ‘demonstrating the impact of doing x versus y’. Supply chains provide a particular challenge in terms of visibility and data Several speakers also highlighted the challenges around visibility of the full supply chain. Shelley Cotterill highlighted that we are currently in a world where we rely on ‘suppliers telling us they are doing the right thing’, whether on the social or environmental front, but we should push for ‘seeing the entire supply chain’, to ‘genuinely’ manage risks. Craig Melson concurred on the challenges but suggested that ‘complete supply chain visibility is a fantasy’. However approaches like shared auditing and the growth of the Responsible Business Alliance offer ways to provide far more transparency and accountability. Even with the right data…assessing the environmental impacts of digital systems is a challenge! As Mike Berners-Lee put it, even with the right data available to us, assessing environmental impact of digital requires we reach a level of ‘system completeness’, and that means we need to consider direct, indirect and rebound effects. For that reason, the technology sector may be the most challenging industry for measuring sustainability impact. Professor Deborah Andrews echoed this point, highlighting that while Lifecycle Assessments are held up as the standard for holistic and rigorous evaluation, they are not an easy activity to conduct, and require specialist and extensive training. As Chris Adams said, we may not all be able to become sustainability experts to that extent, but having training available, to ensure many more of us have enough of understanding these challenges, is important! We also need to make sure that we move beyond ‘carbon tunnel vision’ as it can be misleading Professor Deborah Andrews highlighted that for digital hardware, measuring carbon alone may ‘at best’ be ‘indicative’ of ‘the whole environmental impacts’ of that device, but at worst, it can be misrepresentative. She suggested that, as a rule of thumb, it’s fair to think that carbon (greenhouse gases) from the whole lifecycle represents about 50% of the environmental impact of a product, with the other 50% coming from the materials, through the pollution, resource consumption, land use change etc associated with the mining, processing and manufacturing processes. As Ryan Bark added, while we might not always be able to precisely quantify all of the environmental and social impacts of hardware, we can still ‘tell a story’ about the likely balance, and this is important as it helps raise awareness.
