Choosing a level and order of measurement

Before we can approach measuring the sustainability of a digital service or other project, we need to determine the 'level' and the 'order' of impact that we are assessing.

(i) The 'level' of measurement: whole services or components

Environmental and social impacts can be measured at the component or whole service level. Choose the level that supports the decision you need to make.

Level of measurement Purpose
Whole digital service

Provides a benchmark that a service team can monitor and improve over time. For example, a team could measure greenhouse gas emissions per user or transaction.

Part of a service

Helps teams understand a specific part of the service, such as data storage, software, hosting or user devices.

Practitioners can use the results to identify improvements and assess whether their actions have been successful.

Environmental and social impacts are also frequently measured at the organisational level, for government departments and other public sector bodies. This approach can show whether an organisation's long-term environmental and social strategy is working, and demonstrates transparency and accountability to the public.

(ii) The 'order' of impact of a digital service or project

The orders of impact for a digital service describe how its effects can range from the carbon or water used by digital systems through to the societal and systemic shifts coming from the introduction of new technologies and services.

The orders are usually divided into 3 orders of impact.

Read more about the 'orders' of environmental impacts for digital services
  • ICT for Sustainability – explores how information and communication technologies (ICT) can contribute to sustainability, including their direct environmental impacts and their potential to enable wider reductions in resource use and emissions.
  • UN Trade and Development Digital Economy Report 2024 – examines the environmental impacts of digitalisation across its lifecycle, including demand for energy, water and raw materials, and opportunities to make the digital economy more environmentally sustainable.
Note: Measurement of the environmental and social impacts of digital services and projects should normally focus at the level of first order impacts, as these are within the control of the service. Assessing second order impacts requires measurement against a baseline (such as an existing process or service which is being replaced, in order to determine the 'net' impact of the new service) and assessing third order impacts requires assessment of policy outcomes.

First order impacts ('Direct effects')

These are the direct environmental and social impacts from the lifecycle of ICT hardware and digital infrastructure. They represent costs to the environment and society, and include impacts such as carbon emissions, water, material use, e-waste and supply chain labour risks.

Second order impacts ('Enabling effects')

Second order impacts are the indirect environmental and social changes caused by how digital technology is applied to other sectors and processes. These impacts can be positive (reducing negative environmental impacts) or negative (increasing demand).

Second order impacts are usually described in terms of optimisation, substitution, induction and obsolescence:

Effect Description Example Potential sustainability impact
Optimisation effect Digital technology improves the efficiency of an existing process, activity or system. Smart building controls adjust heating, cooling and lighting according to occupancy and demand. Can reduce energy, materials, water, waste or emissions while delivering the same outcome.
Substitution effect A digital product, service or process replaces a physical activity, product or resource-intensive process. Videoconferencing substitutes for some business travel, while digital documents can replace some printing and physical distribution. Can avoid travel, materials, infrastructure and associated emissions, although the impact of the digital alternative needs to be considered.
Induction effect Digital technology makes an activity easier, cheaper or more accessible, creating additional demand or new patterns of consumption. Streaming services make consuming video content easier, increasing the total amount of content watched and data transferred. Can increase energy, data, infrastructure and resource demand, potentially offsetting efficiency improvements.
Obsolescence effect Digital innovation causes existing products, equipment, skills or processes to become obsolete sooner than they otherwise would. New software or operating-system requirements encourage replacement of otherwise functional devices. Can shorten hardware lifetimes and increase demand for manufacturing, critical raw materials and electronic waste.

Third order impacts

Third-order impacts are the longer-term systemic effects of digital technologies and services that result from changes in behaviour, consumption, business models and society, including rebound effects that can amplify or offset the benefits achieved through optimisation and substitution.

In Defra, these third-order impacts may be explicit policy objectives, such as boosting recycling rates or increasing biodiversity.
